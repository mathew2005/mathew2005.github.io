CSC 426: Managing Risks and Defining Success in the Final Weeks

November 19, 2026

As we enter the final weeks of CSC 426, I have realized that my biggest challenge in open source development is not always writing code. It is managing everything that comes before and after it. At the beginning of the semester, I thought completing an issue mostly meant implementing a solution. Now I understand that testing, reviewing, documenting, and communicating can take just as much time, sometimes more.

Risks and Mitigation Plans

One risk is underestimating the work involved in a contribution. My contributor guide for BCSR was originally estimated at three story points, but I finished two points over. Each review round introduced more work, including documenting different contribution workflows and adding maintainer information. I initially treated review as something that happened after the work was finished. Looking back, that was a mistake. Review is part of the work itself, and I should have accounted for it from the beginning.

I noticed something similar with a small BCSR fix that took weeks to complete. The code change was simple, but testing and reviewing took much longer. These experiences changed how I think about difficulty. A small code change does not necessarily mean a small task.

Another risk is my tendency to delay work when it feels unfinished or complicated. My database credentials pull request is a good example. Every time I opened it, I noticed something else that needed fixing. Instead of breaking those changes into smaller tasks, I kept putting it off. I have noticed this pattern outside programming too. I sometimes let small administrative tasks pile up until they feel much bigger than they originally were.

I think part of the problem is wanting to finish everything at once instead of making steady progress. To address this, I plan to break remaining issues into smaller steps and finish one clear task at a time. I also need to recognize when something is good enough to submit for feedback rather than continuing to make changes on my own.

A third risk is depending on other people's availability. My Octopus pull request has been mergeable since November 14, but it is still waiting for one human review. This is frustrating because the remaining work is outside my control. However, I am learning that submitting a pull request does not automatically mean the contribution is complete.

My plan is to use Thanksgiving break, November 23 to 27, when I have no classes or labor, to work on most of my open issues. I will then use the final two weeks of the semester to get them reviewed and merged. This gives maintainers time to respond and gives me time to address feedback without rushing.

Responsibilities to Users and Contributors

One thing this semester has taught me is that open source contributions affect more people than the developer writing the code. When I fixed the overlapping deadline form fields in BCSR, I was addressing a problem that prevented people using smaller screens from interacting with the form properly.

I also learned that future contributors depend on documentation. My work on CONTRIBUTING.md, SECURITY.md, and CODEOWNERS made me realize that poorly documented projects can create unnecessary work for everyone who joins later.

This changes how I prioritize my remaining work. I want to focus on contributions that improve usability and maintainability rather than choosing issues simply because they are easy to close. I also need to leave enough time for testing and documentation instead of treating them as optional finishing touches.

Progress Update

So far, I have completed 10 estimated story points, with a delta of 3, for a total of 13 story points across six merged pull requests.

Most of the additional effort came from my contributor guide and the responsive form fix. Both taught me that my estimates focused too heavily on coding and underestimated the time needed for review.

Beyond resolved issues, I created a bug report and reviewed pull requests in BCSR and CAS. Reviewing other people's work showed me how important it is to test assumptions instead of assuming a change works because the code looks correct.

Defining Success

By the end of the semester, success means getting my remaining contributions reviewed and merged while maintaining their quality. More importantly, I want to show that I have improved how I manage my work. If I can stop postponing small tasks, account for review time, and submit changes earlier, that will be meaningful progress.

This semester has taught me that being a good open source contributor is not just about solving problems. It is also about making the work easier for the people who come after me.
